# Workshop briefs: independent content review

**Status:** Staging review only. This is not child testing, product approval, or a claim of learning effectiveness. Reviewed 2026-10-26.

## Scope and changes

The supplied batch contained 18 briefs. I retained 9, rejected 9, and revised 7 request texts. The retained briefs are `workshop-a1-01`–`03`, `workshop-a2-01`–`04`, and `workshop-b1-01`–`02`. I removed all three original B2 briefs, all three C1 briefs, and all three C2 briefs. Their advanced labels overstated what was asked of the player: each still resolved to two simple object-to-place choices, and several of the described language features were absent or inaccurately named. For example, original `workshop-b2-02` described “would rather than” and an `it` reference, although its request had neither “than” nor “it.” The initial set also repeated the exact placements in `workshop-a2-01`/`workshop-b1-01` and `workshop-a2-02`/`workshop-b1-02`.

One retained brief, `workshop-a1-01`, now introduces the interaction with just the boat and water. The other eight requests each ask for two different props in two different slots. All nine placement signatures are distinct. The prompts do not tell the player that unmentioned props must stay put or that any slot must be empty; extra decorations remain allowed.

Request texts revised: `workshop-a1-01`, `workshop-a1-02`, `workshop-a1-03`, `workshop-a2-01`, `workshop-a2-02`, `workshop-b1-01`, and `workshop-b1-02`. `workshop-b1-03` was reclassified as A2 and renamed `workshop-a2-04`; its request stayed the same. `workshop-a2-03` also stayed the same. I rewrote the retained help, after-completion, and focus metadata to give a strategy without stating the answer early, describe only requested placements after completion, and name language features that actually occur in the prompt. The B2–C2 items were dropped rather than rewritten as artificial advanced filler.

The bands are rough editorial estimates of the English a reader encounters, based on vocabulary, sentence length, clause links, and reference tracking. They are not formal CEFR matches or proficiency results. A1 briefs use concrete object and place words with short direct requests; A2 briefs add features such as a pronoun, a reported request, a short reason clause, or a discourse marker; the two B1 briefs combine clause links with a backward reference or purpose phrase. Given the project context that the existing Atlas already has 120 A1–C2 activities, this workshop batch does not need to manufacture advanced labels to fill the range. CEFR labels do not establish that a task suits a particular age. The Council of Europe descriptors do not provide a calibration for this toy or a CEFR age mapping for children aged 4–5.

## Learning-format comparison and limits

A construction toy makes object-and-place choices visible and lets a player act on them. With a responsive adult naming objects, following the child's attention, and talking about placements, it can support shared practice. A screen that accepts placements by itself is not necessarily guided play; the interaction and adult support matter. The six props and four slots here provide a small practice setting, not a measure of broad vocabulary or language ability.

A timed arcade format puts speed and rapid feedback in the foreground. A branching narrative can put more weight on following a story, reading or listening over time, and choosing between consequences. These are differences in task demands, not an effectiveness ranking. The cited studies do not compare this harbor toy with a timed game or branching narrative, and none tests this app or these prompts.

Research offers limited, outcome-specific context. Fisher et al. taught 4- and 5-year-olds geometric properties through guided play, free play, or direct instruction; 60 children were included in the analyses. Guided-play participants showed stronger shape knowledge than the other groups, including at a one-week follow-up. This was a short, small study of geometric concepts, not English vocabulary, a digital toy, or independent screen use. In Toub et al., Study 1 included 249 preschoolers (mean age about 59 months); vocabulary was introduced through shared book-reading and then revisited in play conditions. The study found gains for taught vocabulary and an advantage for adult-supported play over free play in that intervention. It does not show that these harbor prompts teach English, transfer to children learning English as an additional language, or work without the shared-reading and adult-guidance elements of the study. Neither result justifies promising an outcome for this product.

If the game is offered to pre-readers or children around ages 4–5, invite an adult to read the English request and support the play. A child may respond by tapping, pointing, or moving an object; do not require reading, typing, or speaking. An adult-read prompt changes the task to supported practice: a correct placement is not evidence of independent reading, listening, or CEFR proficiency. The bilingual help and after text can support an adult, but they have not been reviewed with families or early-childhood and bilingual specialists.

## Review boundary

This was a desk review of the brief data and cited research. I did not test the prompts with children, families, teachers, or English-language specialists; inspect illustrations or audio assets; or evaluate the runtime interaction. Before child-facing use, have the English and Vietnamese checked by competent bilingual reviewers and the activity reviewed with early-childhood practitioners. Any later learning claim needs evidence from the actual activity and intended learners. Keep the current description to adult-supported visual vocabulary practice.
