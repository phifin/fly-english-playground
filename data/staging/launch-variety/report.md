## Coverage

The pack contains 12 original, all-ages missions: two per level from A1 through C2, with 60 activities total. Every mission has one choice, one gap, one match, one order and one reflective writing activity. The first four types are objectively gradable; writing remains self-review only.

The mission skill is reading because each mission uses a meaningful English text. Individual language activities test a constrained form, and writing activities are original models with two-point self-review rubrics. Personal choices appear as optional, open prompts; the stories do not assume a learner's age, job, travel or life experience.

The six regions are Lantern Harbor (A1), Wildseed Gardens (A2), Tideglass Isles (B1), Clockwork Canopy (B2), Prism Observatory (C1) and the Unwritten Sky (C2). Their situations range from personal color and toy choices to comparative garden sculptures, creative invitations, narrative discoveries, editorial genres, formal program notes and subtle literary interpretation. This variety avoids the earlier batches' map errands, schedules, rule checks and evidence reports.

## Answer rationale and ambiguity review

Choice distractors below are intentionally unsupported or conflict with the stated context. Gap alternatives are listed in full. Match answers map each left-hand item to its unique right-hand item. Every order activity has one required sequence and no additional accepted permutation; prompts state a time sequence or rhetorical structure. Writing models are examples, not keys.

### A1 — The Color-Teller Lantern

1. **Choice — yellow.** Niko says he likes yellow and keeps that lantern for his window. Green belongs to Lela; blue is not mentioned as a choice.
2. **Gap — it.** The task explicitly asks for the subject pronoun replacing one singular object, the paper tail.
3. **Match — Rin → silver; Omar → green; Tia → blue.** Each person directly names a different color.
4. **Order — choose a paper color → fold the paper → draw a moon.** The note's first/next/last labels give the sole order.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** The example describes the writer's own color choice and action. The rubric checks a preference and present-tense subject/verb use; no objective answer is scored.

### A1 — The Rooftop Kite Club

1. **Choice — stripes.** Sami names stripes as his choice; dots are Jo's choice, and clouds are not selected.
2. **Gap — they.** The task asks for the plural subject pronoun replacing two names.
3. **Match — red ribbon → tie it to the tail; green circle → draw it on the sail; two bells → add them to the frame.** The making note connects each part to one action.
4. **Order — choose a color → fold two sheets → add the tail.** First/next/last fixes the sequence.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** Any suitable toy or game is allowed. The rubric checks that the learner names a preference and uses a simple present action verb.

### A2 — The Taller Seed Sculpture

1. **Choice — tall-acorn.** The question asks for the tallest sculpture: Tall Acorn is 31 cm, Paper Moon 22 cm and Wide Nest 18 cm. Only Tall Acorn has the greatest measured height.
2. **Gap — flowers.** The task specifies the countable noun flower and asks for its plural after five.
3. **Match - many pairs with seed pods; a little with soil; a few with clay bowls.** The task asks learners to recover the specific pairings in the making note; both many and a few can grammatically modify plural count nouns.
4. **Order — The spiral frame → is wider → than → the round frame.** The tokens form the exact comparison sentence; no alternate word order follows the prompt.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** Learners select two objects or materials. The rubric checks a clear comparison and suitable quantity language.

### A2 — An Invitation to Make a Sound Garden

1. **Choice — polite.** “Would you mind helping …?” asks politely; the other options give an order or describe ability.
2. **Gap — would.** The blank is restricted to the modal in the fixed “Would you mind + -ing” question form.
3. **Match — Could you carry …? → polite request; Would you like to join …? → invitation; We could make … → suggestion.** Each message has a distinct interaction purpose.
4. **Order — Would you like to → paint → a leaf pattern → with us?** This produces the invitation's standard question order; the prompt requires that exact question.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** Learners may invite someone to any creative activity. The rubric checks a polite question/suggestion and a stated plan.

### B1 — The Moon-Moth Model

1. **Choice — light.** Eni leaves the wings unpainted so projector light can pass through them; the alternatives are not supported.
2. **Gap - two words before the printed to.** The blank forms a three-word purpose phrase ending in the supplied to; accepted alternatives are in order and so as.
3. **Match — leave wings unpainted → let light pass through; move lamp closer → make shadow grow; ask Jo to trace → add outline to story wall.** The story gives one purpose for each action.
4. **Order — make paper moth → blue beam lights wings → move lamp → Jo traces shape.** The narrative's first/then/after/finally cues give the only order.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** The rubric checks a past-time sequence and an explicit reason for the character's changed plan.

### B1 — The Borrowed Tide Song

1. **Choice — future.** Tavi leaves the line open so a future singer can add another sound; the other purposes conflict with the note.
2. **Gap — to.** The one-word infinitive marker introduces the stated purpose of opening the notebook.
3. **Match — borrow recording → remember original tune; sit by sea grass → compare clicks with melody; leave line open → invite future contribution.** Each intention is explicitly attached to a different action.
4. **Order — borrow recording → play it beside grass → notice clicks → write verse.** Tavi hears the unexpected clicks before composing the new verse.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** The rubric checks the new detail, the character's response and the reason for changing a plan.

### B2 — A Listening Guide in Three Voices

1. **Choice — metaphor.** The option attributes the statement to Leta and preserves her contrast between imitation and giving the wind a vocabulary. The other options reverse, literalise or replace her view.
2. **Gap — were tuning.** The prompt requires standard backshift from present continuous to past continuous; the Monday remark is reported in a Tuesday bulletin, and the plural subject is explicit.
3. **Match — draw a distinction → separate related ideas; lend weight to a reading → make an interpretation more persuasive; take issue with a label → disagree with its wording or claim.** The collocations have distinct functions in the context.
4. **Order — cover claim → maker's image → reviewer's response.** The style note names that exact rhetorical progression.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** Learners attribute a maker's view and separate it from their own response. The rubric also checks a suitable collocation.

### B2 — Is It a Review or a Manual?

1. **Choice — review.** The sentence evaluates the reader's experience; the other options instruct the reader to operate or handle an object.
2. **Gap — air.** The blank is restricted to the final noun in the fixed collocation “a breath of fresh air.”
3. **Match — review → evaluates experience; field manual → explains use/handling; catalogue blurb → briefly introduces work.** Each genre has one distinct purpose in the booklet.
4. **Order — initial response → supporting detail → final judgement.** The editor's style note directly specifies this review structure.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** The rubric checks that the text evaluates rather than instructs and supports the evaluation with a detail and collocation.

### C1 — The Glass Choir

1. **Choice — concession.** The sentence acknowledges the score's brevity but retains its positive overall effect. The other options remove or contradict that relationship.
2. **Gap - accepted: even though.** The prompt specifies a two-word concessive linker that treats short notes as an established fact, not a hypothetical condition.
3. **Match — despite → noun phrase; although → full clause; whereas → two contrasting clauses.** Each phrase is followed by its named structure in the program.
4. **Order — Only after the final note faded → did → the prism → settle into blue.** The prompt requests the fronted “Only after … did …” construction; no other permutation preserves its inversion.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** The rubric checks that the note acknowledges a feature and keeps its main evaluation clear.

### C1 — The Program Editor's Pencil

1. **Choice — formal.** The selected revision uses measured evaluative wording suitable for a program; the other choices remain conversational or vague.
2. **Gap — did.** The past retrospective and the prompt's past-simple inversion requirement make did the intended auxiliary; the main verb remains in base form.
3. **Match - this motif refers to the earlier circle motif; whereas signals clause contrast; in the final room locates recurrence.** These textual functions differ.
4. **Order — open-circle topic → single-line contrast → motif synthesis.** The prompt fixes topic, contrast and synthesis in that order, without suggesting a causal link.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** The rubric checks preserved meaning, formal register and one cohesive connective/reference.

### C2 — The Museum of Almosts

1. **Choice — qualified-censure.** The narrator's polite qualification makes the “apology” label sound generous beyond what the note clearly accepts, while the final line questions the cost of the museum's praise. The other options overstate sincerity, reverse the stated chronology or accuse the apprentice without support.
2. **Gap — equivocal.** The prompt asks for the adjective beginning with e for wording open to two readings; the caption neither endorses nor directly criticises the omission.
3. **Match — tidy, if a shade too eager → qualified praise with mild reservation; triumph by every measure → unreserved praise; eight panels in two rooms → neutral factual description.** The wording marks distinct evaluative stances.
4. **Order — visible description → generous-sounding interpretation → qualification.** The editor's plan states the required rhetorical sequence.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** Learners distinguish a stated feature from an interpretation and use a qualifier to set its scope.

The literary context in activity 01 is **185 words**. Its question asks for the pragmatic force of qualified praise, and the distractors represent plausible overreadings rather than a simple logic puzzle or a test of a famous idiom.

### C2 — The Editor's Pencil

1. **Choice — reader-effect.** “Breathing space” figuratively gives the blank a possible function for a reader, while the editor explicitly avoids attributing that effect to the poet's documented intention. The other choices literalise, overstate or deny the page's visible feature.
2. **Gap - accepted: figuratively, metaphorically, non-literally, nonliterally.** Each describes the caption's non-literal wording.
3. **Match — could be read as an invitation → leaves interpretation open; nothing more than a pause → reduces significance; serves as a welcome pause for some readers → positive but limited evaluation.** The language signals distinct degrees and directions of stance.
4. **Order - physical description, possible reader effect, limit on intention.** The last chunk states that the caption does not claim the poet intended the reader's response.
5. **Writing — reflective only.** The rubric checks the observable fact and whether an unrecorded intention is kept distinct from interpretation.

## Educational limits and validation

The level labels identify intended content bands only. This batch is a short original library, not a representative assessment; its items do not establish a learner's CEFR level. The CEFR Companion Volume offers illustrative descriptors, but the activities have not been calibrated with target-level learners, independently reviewed or tested for accessibility. C1/C2 nuance and difficulty are author judgements. Writing receives no automatic correctness or CEFR credit.

Content is all-ages and does not assume advanced proficiency implies adult experience. Short-choice and constrained tasks mainly assess recognition, interpretation and selected language forms. They do not measure spontaneous speaking, sustained independent writing, listening or transfer to unseen contexts. The order items rely on explicit sequence/rhetorical instructions; narrative activities connect reasons only where the story states them, not from timing alone. The B2 report task explicitly requires standard backshift to avoid alternate present-tense readings.
